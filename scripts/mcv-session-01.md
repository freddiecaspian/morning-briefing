# MCV Session 1 - Customer Value: Concept, Application, Measurement

Tuesday the tenth of March. This one's a bit different from the usual morning briefing. You've got your first Managing Customer Value session coming up with Professor Paulo Albuquerque, and there's a decent amount of ground to cover. So let's walk through the lecture material and the two readings properly, the way you'd want a sharp friend to explain it the night before.

The big idea of the whole course sits in one equation. Customer Value equals Perceived Benefits minus Perceived Costs. Simple on the surface, but there's a lot hiding in the word "perceived." Value doesn't live in the product. It lives in the customer's head. Two people can look at the exact same thing and arrive at completely different valuations. And that's not irrational. It's just human. The entire course is basically the applied version of understanding, measuring, and capturing what goes on in that gap between reality and perception.

So let's break the benefits side down. Albuquerque lays out four types of consumer benefits, and they'll keep coming back throughout the fourteen sessions, so it's worth getting them lodged in properly.

First, economic benefits. This is the spreadsheet dimension. Savings, efficiency, return on investment, cost per use. When someone picks the budget airline or the basic SaaS tier, they're optimising here. The pricing logic that follows is cost-plus with competitive anchoring. Straightforward, rational, and often where engineers and finance people feel most comfortable.

Second, functional benefits. Does it work? Does it work better, faster, or more reliably than the alternative? This is performance, features, reliability. It's where product teams tend to spend most of their energy, and it's the dimension where you'll see value-based pricing tied to ROI or total cost of ownership calculations. Enterprise software, medical devices - that territory.

Third, experiential benefits. This is the hedonic dimension. How does it look, feel, taste, sound? Sensory pleasure. And it becomes increasingly important in categories where functional parity has been reached. When every phone has roughly the same specs, differentiation has to come from delight rather than specification. The pricing logic here flips - prestige pricing, where the price itself becomes part of the signal.

And fourth, social benefits. Status, identity, belonging. The product signals group membership, elevates social standing, or enables self-expression. This is where brands like Apple and Patagonia and luxury goods extract their premium. The pricing model is exclusivity - supply caps, limited editions, events. And this one is going to come roaring back when we get to the Birkin bag reading in a few minutes.

Now, these four aren't mutually exclusive. A single product delivers value across multiple dimensions simultaneously, and different customer segments weight them differently. Understanding which benefit type is primary for your target segment is what drives your positioning, your pricing, and your communication strategy. That's really the strategic task of the course.

There's a lovely case study in the lecture that makes this concrete. The avocado. Yes, the avocado. Before the 2000s, it was a niche commodity at about fifty cents a unit. Good experiential benefit, but high experiential cost because you had to wait for ripeness. Then between 2000 and 2010, the functional benefit got unlocked through the healthy fats narrative. Chipotle drove guacamole adoption. From 2010 to 2018, avocado toast appears on cafe menus at fourteen to twenty-two dollars. Experiential plus social benefits fully activated. Price premium of three hundred percent versus the commodity. Avocados From Mexico even ran a Super Bowl ad in 2015. And from 2018 onwards, full segmentation: organic, single-origin, Hass versus other varietals. Multiple price tiers from eighty cents to four dollars plus per unit. The market grew to four billion dollars. And the critical thing is - the physical product didn't change. Marketers systematically activated new benefit layers for new segments, and each layer justified a higher price point and expanded the addressable market.

Now let's talk about the gap between value created and value captured. Because they're not the same thing. Pricing strategy is what closes that gap. The lecture uses a mobile gaming example to illustrate this, and the segmentation is quite vivid. You've got whales, dolphins, and minnows. Whales are the smallest group of players but generate over seventy percent of revenue. Dolphins spend moderately. Minnows are the largest group but spend the least. The mechanism for bridging that gap is price discrimination - charging different users different prices based on their willingness to pay. In practice, that means pricing tiers, bundles, and versions. All forms of indirect price discrimination. And the gaming insight connects right back to the social benefit layer - the reason whales pay so much is partly social signalling and status within the game community. Mobile content becomes a luxury item.

This leads to a neat table worth remembering. Economic benefits lead to cost-plus pricing with competitive anchoring. Functional benefits lead to value-based pricing using ROI and total cost of ownership. Experiential benefits lead to prestige pricing where the price is the signal. And social benefits lead to exclusivity pricing with supply caps and events. Most successful companies price differently across segments, capturing value from each benefit layer simultaneously.

Alright, let's shift to measurement. How do you actually measure what customers value? Albuquerque makes a sharp distinction between revealed preferences and stated preferences. Revealed preferences are what people actually do - their behaviour in natural settings. Stated preferences are what people say they'd do in surveys or lab settings. And the problem with stated preferences is that respondents behave strategically once they know they're being measured. The gold standard for causal identification comes from natural experiments, clickstream data, and A/B tests. Revealed over stated, always.

The key analytical tool here is conjoint analysis. Rather than asking customers directly what they value, which invites strategic responses, conjoint presents people with complete product profiles varying across attributes and asks them to choose. By analysing the pattern of choices, you can mathematically decompose how much each attribute contributes to overall preference. These are called part-worth utilities. Apple actually used conjoint analysis in its billion-dollar patent case against Samsung to quantify the dollar value of specific design features. And machine learning now makes this scalable - you can run conjoint at ten thousand respondents, including synthetic respondents, though that's contested.

The lecture had a nice little exercise. Four food vendors at a stadium. Garlic fries with one person in line, one minute wait, six fifty. Pizza slice, one person, one minute, three fifty. Clam chowder, five people in line, five minutes, five dollars. Hot dog, three people, three minutes, five dollars. Then the follow-up question: what if the pizza price went up to five dollars? Or six fifty? The switching point reveals true willingness to pay. That's conjoint logic in miniature.

Right. Now let's get into the readings. The first one is brilliant. The Economics of the Birkin Bag. It's an NPR Planet Money episode, and it's a masterclass in how social benefits and artificial scarcity can completely invert normal commerce.

Here's the setup. The Birkin bag is a handbag by Hermes. They average around sixty thousand dollars. You can get a starter one for about ten thousand. And they're always mysteriously out of stock. Hermes claims it's because they're hand-sewn by select artisans in southern France using exotic leathers, taking twelve hours per bag. Josh Weltman, an advertising producer who worked on Mad Men, dismisses this entirely. After thirty years, Hermes could easily have scaled production. The scarcity is a deliberate marketing strategy, not a supply constraint.

And then Hermes goes further than just scarcity. Where most stores try to make you feel welcome, Hermes's strategy is to reject and humiliate. Weltman calls it hazing. You get waitlisted for two to four years. There are rumours of waitlists for the waitlist. And the psychology is deliberate - once you're in, the humiliation creates a bond and a sense of identity. The mechanism mirrors fraternity initiation or elite college admissions. The harder it is to join, the more valuable membership feels.

There's a wonderful story from Wednesday Martin, who moved to the Upper East Side and had a woman use her Birkin to physically walk her into a garbage can on the sidewalk. A literal dominance display using a handbag. And Martin's response wasn't "these people are crazy, I need to move." It was "I need one of those bags." The bag restructured her social position in the neighbourhood. People who didn't have Birkins approached her to ask how to get one. People who did wanted to exchange war stories. The bag functioned as social infrastructure, not just consumption.

Here's the economic tension that makes this really interesting for the course. Every time someone walks in and asks for a Birkin and Hermes says no, that's a sixty-thousand-dollar sale they're deliberately forgoing. Weltman frames it as "they don't get greedy." Short-term revenue sacrifice sustains long-term brand premium. That's what's kept the Birkin at the top for three decades while competitors who chased volume diluted their cachet.

And then there's Michael Tonello, who accidentally cracked the code. He bought thousands of dollars of Hermes scarves first, then casually asked for a Birkin. And it worked. Every time. He describes it as chemistry - the order in which you put the chemicals into the beaker matters. What happened is reciprocity. After the big scarf purchase, the sales staff felt grateful, felt they owed him something, and when he asked at that moment, the normal human instinct to reciprocate momentarily overrode the scarcity protocol.

Now, map all of this back to the lecture framework. The Birkin is the ultimate example of social benefits driving pricing. The value equation is almost entirely perception-based. The functional and experiential benefits of the bag are, by the reporter's own admission, underwhelming. It's boxy, plain, the leather doesn't even seem that nice. But the social benefit is so powerful that it overrides everything else. And the pricing model is pure exclusivity - supply caps, artificial scarcity, hazing rituals. This is what Albuquerque means when he says different benefit types drive different pricing logic.

The second reading takes us in a completely different direction. It's a webinar from the Marketing Science Institute featuring Johannes Habel and Bill Donley from Penske Transportation Solutions, and it's a live demonstration of using ChatGPT across the entire marketing and sales process.

The setup is that Habel role-plays as a newly hired CMO at Penske and uses ChatGPT to work through market research, innovation, branding, communications, and sales prospecting in real time. Donley, who actually works at Penske, validates every output against industry reality.

There are a few key things worth remembering for the session. First, ChatGPT correctly identified driver shortages and retention as the most prevalent and least addressed customer pain point. Donley confirmed this. That maps directly to the value equation from the lecture - ChatGPT is essentially surfacing customer pain points, which are costs on the perceived costs side, and then generating innovations, which are benefit additions.

Second, the iterative refinement process is crucial. When ChatGPT generated brand names for a new autonomous convoy product, they didn't fit Penske's naming conventions. But when Donley gave that feedback and it was relayed to ChatGPT, the model adapted and produced names that fitted. Donley selected "Penske Smart Convoy." The workflow pattern is: the LLM generates, the expert evaluates against tacit knowledge, the LLM adapts. Neither party could efficiently do the other's job.

Third, and this is important, hallucination is practical, not just theoretical. In a thirty-minute demo, ChatGPT produced a wrong phone number for a product flyer, suggested mismatched sales prospects - oil and gas companies for a long-haul trucking solution - and initially targeted the wrong decision-makers at Cisco. Domain expertise was essential at every step to redirect the tool.

The fundamental value proposition Habel articulates is "it's easier to edit than it is to create." The LLM isn't expected to produce ready-to-use output. It produces first drafts that reduce the cognitive burden of starting from a blank page. And the real value emerges through progressive refinement with domain feedback, not single-shot queries.

There's also a practical enterprise angle. Penske licenses a model behind their own firewall rather than using public ChatGPT, trains it on internal documents like maintenance manuals and logs, and uses it as an internal subject matter expert. Technicians query it for repair procedures, warranty agreements, parts recalls. That's the next level beyond ad hoc querying.

Now, how do these two readings connect to each other and back to the lecture? They're almost opposite case studies along the benefit spectrum. The Birkin bag is pure social benefit, where the value is almost entirely perception and status, and the pricing is exclusivity-driven. The Penske ChatGPT demo is pure functional benefit - operational efficiency, cost reduction, process acceleration - with cost-plus and value-based pricing logic. One is B2C luxury at its most extreme, the other is B2B industrial at its most practical. But both illustrate the same core equation: Customer Value equals Perceived Benefits minus Perceived Costs. They just activate completely different benefit types.

Let me wrap up with the four key takeaways from Session 1 that Albuquerque flags as the ones to carry forward.

One. Customer value equals perceived benefits minus perceived costs. Price follows value, not cost. Get that equation lodged permanently.

Two. Benefits come in four flavours - economic, functional, experiential, and social. The strategic task is finding the best combination for your product and segment.

Three. Different segments lead to different demand curves. Price premiums are achievable, but only when value is well perceived and communicated. The avocado didn't change. The perception did.

And four. Measure behaviour, not intent. Build insights on revealed preferences. Conjoint analysis is the key tool, and machine learning makes it scalable.

Next session moves into market segmentation - who is your real customer? Which builds directly on the "different segments, different demand curves" insight from today.

That's your twenty-five minutes on MCV Session 1. You've got the equation, the four benefit types, the pricing logic, the measurement tools, and two readings that sit at opposite ends of the benefit spectrum but prove the same point. Go well.
